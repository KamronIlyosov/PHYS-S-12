Electricity Generating Leg Brace

A wearable that turns walking into usable electricity, with a real circuit, a real gear reduction, and a 3D printed calf mount.

Executive summary

My final project is a wearable walking device that harvests mechanical energy from leg motion and converts it to electrical energy. It uses a stepper motor as the generator, 2 full bridge rectifiers (one per coil), 2 smoothing capacitors, a pulley reduction system with about a 5.88:1 ratio, a 3D printed calf mount, and 2 laser cut acrylic plates that form the chassis. The goal was to build a repeatable working prototype that proves the concept of human motion energy harvesting, while documenting clearly what still needs to happen for a future version to actually charge a phone reliably (energy buffer, buck/boost converter with proper minimum input handling, real bearings instead of stacked washers).

Motivation: I care a lot about environmental preservation, and the idea of generating electricity from movement that's already happening is one of the most appealing applications of that. Walking is the most common form of human motion, and most of the mechanical energy from it just gets dissipated as heat in joints and muscles. So if a small wearable can grab even a fraction of that energy and put it somewhere useful, that's a small contribution toward less reliance on the grid and on batteries that get charged from it.

Design files and CAD notes

Everything mechanical was designed in Fusion 360. The pulleys and calf mount got exported as STLs for 3D printing, the acrylic plates got exported as DXFs for the laser cutter. The labels I used for the files are below.

plate_front.dxf — laser cut front plate

Has the motor mount holes, the clearance hole for the small pulley, and 4 corner holes for the chassis bolts.

plate_back.dxf — laser cut back plate

Same shape as the front plate but without the motor cutouts. The back plate has the engraved label.

pulley_17T.stl — small pulley (17 teeth)

Mounts onto the motor shaft, this is the small driven pulley. Locked to the shaft with set screws.

pulley_100T.stl — large pulley (100 teeth)

Sits on the calf axle. As the leg bends, this pulley rotates, which through the belt drives the small pulley on the motor a lot faster.

calf_mount.stl — 3D printed calf cradle

The part that actually touches the leg. Shaped to follow the curve of the calf with cutouts for the velcro to loop through.

Fabrication and build steps

Step 1 — Laser cut acrylic plates
  1. Exported plate_front.dxf and plate_back.dxf out of Fusion. The front plate has motor mount holes, pulley clearance, and 4 corner holes for the bolts that hold the 2 plates together. The back plate is plain except for the engraved "Kamrons generator" label on one side.
  2. Material: 3 mm acrylic. Engrave side up on the laser bed.
  3. Ran a small test cut first to dial in the power and speed for 3mm acrylic, then ran the real cut. Peeled the protective film off both sides.
  4. Fitment: the motor mount holes came out a little tight, so I reamed them out with a drill bit one size up. After that the motor dropped right in.
Step 2 — 3D print the pulleys and the calf mount
  1. The gear ratio is the most important mechanical decision in this whole project. A walking pace bends the knee maybe 1 or 2 times per second, but the motor needs hundreds or thousands of RPM to make real voltage. So I went with a 17-tooth small pulley and a 100-tooth large pulley, which gives a 100/17 ≈ 5.88:1 ratio. Every full rotation of the calf axle drives the motor shaft about 5.88 times.
  2. Exported the STLs and double checked the shaft bore matched the motor shaft diameter, because if the pulley is too loose the set screws can't grip and if it's too tight the pulley won't slide on at all.
  3. Print settings, 20–30% infill, supports on the overhangs. The calf mount itself took about 10 hours to print because it's a big curved part.
  4. My first calf mount print failed about halfway through, the brim lifted and the next layers got skewed. I retuned the printer settings with Bobby in the lab and the second print came out clean.
  5. After printing I removed the supports, sanded the surfaces that touch the leg so they wouldn't rub, and dry fit everything before glueing or bolting anything.
Step 3 — Pulleys, motor, and calf mount assembly
  1. Small pulley to motor shaft:
    1. Slid the 17T pulley onto the shaft. Tightened the M4 set screws while holding the shaft steady so the pulley didn't shift mid tightening.
  1. Big pulley and calf axle:
    1. The calf axle is just a long bolt. The 100T pulley sits on that bolt with washers on either side to space it correctly.
    2. To anchor the bolt I ran it through the calf mount (washer on top), then through the front acrylic plate, then through the back plate, and finally a nut on the far side to lock it. Lots of washers in between to keep everything aligned and free to rotate.
    3. I used way too many washers because I didn't have a proper shaft collar or keyed coupling, so it's all shim stack instead of a real fastening. In the next version I want to replace this with a shaft collar or a printed clamp, but for the demo the washers work.
  1. Hand tested the whole thing by spinning the big pulley and watching the small one. Made small adjustments to washer count until the belt path was right and nothing was binding.
  2. The calf mount kept slipping on the bolt during walking tests, because the washer stack isn't a real grip. I hot glued the calf mount to the bolt as a temporary fix. It's a band aid, see the improvements section for what I want to do properly next time.
Step 4 — Belt transmission
  1. Looped the belt around both pulleys. The distance between the pulleys (the "center to center" distance) controls belt tension, too close and the belt is too loose and slips, too far and it's too tight and the motor can't turn it. I adjusted the chassis spacing until the tension felt right.
  2. To check the gear ratio by hand, I rotated the calf pulley one full turn and counted motor shaft rotations. Got about 5.88, which matches the math (100 / 17 ≈ 5.88).
  3. Spin test, slowly turned the calf pulley while a multimeter was on the motor coils in AC mode. Got real AC voltage showing up, which proved the mechanical chain was working before I started on the electronics.

Wiring and circuit

A stepper motor has 2 coil pairs (it's a 2 phase machine), so when the shaft spins, each coil pair generates its own AC signal. I handled them separately, one bridge rectifier per coil pair, with a smoothing capacitor on each rectifier's DC output. The 2 DC rails can then be combined into one to drive a load (in my demo, 18 LEDs).

The big picture

  1. Stepper coil A (2 wires) → bridge rectifier A AC inputs → DC output → 100µF cap → common DC rail
  2. Stepper coil B (2 wires) → bridge rectifier B AC inputs → DC output → 100µF cap → common DC rail
  3. Common DC rail → load (LEDs for the demo, or a converter for charging in a future version)

Conceptual diagram

Motor coil A (2 wires)  ----> Full bridge rectifier A  ----> +DC (100µF cap) ----> common DC rail (to load)
Motor coil B (2 wires)  ----> Full bridge rectifier B  ----> +DC (100µF cap) ----> common DC rail (to load)

Why a buck/boost didn't work for me

I tried hooking the common DC rail into a buck/boost converter that would step the voltage to a steady 5V (for USB charging). The problem is my generator's output is unsteady, when I'm walking slowly the voltage can drop below 3V, which is below the converter's minimum input. When the converter loses its minimum, it either shuts off or starts spiking the output as it tries to boost from nothing, which is bad for whatever's downstream. So for the demo I dropped the converter and just lit the LEDs directly off the rectified + smoothed rail. The LEDs are forgiving, they light up at whatever brightness the current voltage supports, and they don't care about ripple.

Soldering it together

  1. Each coil pair got soldered to its own bridge rectifier's AC inputs. Confirmed continuity on both AC and DC sides before powering anything up.
  2. Soldered a 100 µF electrolytic capacitor across the + and - rails of each rectifier (with the polarity right, otherwise the cap can explode).
  3. Soldered an LED + resistor across the combined DC rail and spun the motor. The LED lit. That was the moment the whole thing finally worked end to end.
  4. Moved the whole circuit from a breadboard to an "Electro Cookie" solderable protoboard, because the breadboard was getting unreliable, wires kept popping out when I moved the device around.

What I'd add to the wiring in version 2

  • An energy buffer (supercap or small Li-ion + a charge controller) so the converter sees a steady input even when the generator output dips.
  • TVS diodes or a Zener clamp on the converter input so voltage spikes don't kill the downstream electronics.
  • Schottky diodes to OR-join the 2 rectifier outputs instead of just tying them together, which would prevent one coil from back-feeding into the other.

Wiring sanity checklist (do this before applying power)

  1. Rectifier polarity right? AC pins on coil leads, + and - on the DC side.
  2. Capacitor polarity right? Long leg to +, short / striped leg to -.
  3. Multimeter on diode check, no shorted diodes inside the bridge.
  4. Hand spin first, confirm DC voltage at the rail with no load, then add load and re-measure.

Testing and measurements

Once everything was assembled and soldered, the testing was straightforward.

Procedure

  1. Multimeter in AC mode across each coil pair, manually spin the motor to confirm AC generation. Bobby helped with this part because spinning fast enough by hand while holding a multimeter on tiny terminals is awkward.
  2. Switch the multimeter to DC and measure after the rectifier and the smoothing cap. This is the no load DC voltage.
  3. Add a known load (a resistor or a bank of LEDs) and measure again. This is the loaded DC voltage.
  4. The difference between no load and loaded gives a rough sense of how much current the generator can deliver before the voltage starts to sag.

Final assembly, finishing, and comfort

  1. Once the mechanism and electronics were both working, I hot glued the protoboard between the 2 acrylic plates, that way the wiring lives inside the sandwich and the outside of the device looks clean.
  2. For comfort against the leg, I glued a piece of soft silicone pad to the inside curve of the 3D printed calf mount. Without it the printed PLA edges dug into the skin a little. With the pad on, walking around for several minutes was actually fine.
  1. For the strap, I added 2 bolts through the acrylic plate as velcro anchors. The velcro loops through built in velcro holders I'd modeled into the calf mount, so when it's strapped on it's pulling against the right point on the mount, not on a random piece of plastic.
  1. Closed the second acrylic plate with the corner bolts and nuts. Walked around the lab slowly to make sure everything was secure and the generator was producing.

Safety notes and PPE

  • Eye protection for the laser cutter, the drill, and any sanding.
  • Electrolytic caps explode if you wire them backwards. Always check polarity before powering up.
  • Solder in a ventilated area, the flux fumes are not great.
  • Hot glue and silicone can stick to skin. Use the back of a knife to peel off if it does.
  • Do not plug an actual phone into a generator like this without proper charging circuitry and surge protection in between. The output isn't clean enough for sensitive electronics.

Lessons learned, known issues, and improvements

Things that went wrong

  • Laser cut holes came out a little tight, fixed by reaming them out with a drill, but I'd dial in laser settings better next time.
  • First calf mount print failed (brim lift). Bobby helped me retune and the second print was fine.
  • Calf mount slipping on the axle bolt was a constant headache. Hot glue is what I ended up doing, and it's not a real fix.
  • Buck boost converter wouldn't run when the input dropped below 3V (which happened often at walking pace), so I had to drop it from the build and run LEDs directly instead. That's why the demo proves the concept but doesn't actually charge a phone yet.
  • Couldn't get a usable NEMA 17 gearbox after a couple of failed prints, and the off the shelf ones were over budget, so I built my own pulley reduction. Worked, but a precision gearbox would be tighter mechanically.

Short term fixes (easy next time)

  • Use a shaft collar or a keyed hub to fasten the calf mount to the axle, instead of hot glue and washers.
  • Replace the washer stack with sized printed spacers, or with actual bearings if the budget allows.
  • Add Schottky diodes between each rectifier output and the common rail so the coils can't fight each other.

Bigger version 2 ideas

  • An energy buffer (supercap or small Li-ion + a real charge controller) so the buck/boost sees a steady input even when the leg slows down.
  • A generator wound for low speed, low torque human motion specifically, instead of repurposing a stepper. The stepper works but it's a compromise.
  • Real bearings on the calf axle to cut friction, more energy in instead of lost to drag.
  • Add a microcontroller back into the build to log energy per step, total steps, and calories, like my Week 7 MVP did, with a small OLED on the side of the device.

Summary

This documents the whole path from concept to a working demo, the laser cut chassis, the 3D printed pulleys and calf mount, the 5.88:1 pulley reduction, the dual bridge rectifier smoothing, the wiring decisions, and the testing. The prototype works as a demonstration unit, walking with it on lights up 18 LEDs at a solid brightness, which proves the energy harvesting concept end to end. It also shows the real limitations clearly, the voltage is unsteady, the buck/boost converter needs a steady input I can't yet give it, and the mechanical fastening needs proper hardware. Those are the things I'd fix in a version 2, and I have a fairly clear plan for each of them.
